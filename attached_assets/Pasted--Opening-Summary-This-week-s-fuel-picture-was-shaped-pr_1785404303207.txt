### Opening Summary

This week’s fuel picture was shaped primarily by continued disruption to Russian refining capacity following drone strikes. Although officials reported that several refineries were returning to operation and conditions were stabilising, fresh interruptions and continued petrol imports indicate that recovery remains uneven. The immediate risk has shifted away from rapid deterioration, but fuel continuity remains exposed where restarted capacity is not yet translating into reliable wholesale and retail supply.

### Market Read

Brent closed the period at approximately USD 92.93/bbl and WTI at USD 85.57/bbl. Both benchmarks declined by more than 7% over seven days but remain elevated, indicating that the immediate geopolitical premium has eased without disappearing. U.S. Gulf Coast jet fuel ended the period at USD 3.582/gal, 0.9% above its 20 July level.

The combined picture is one of persistent, rather than accelerating, cost pressure. Fuel-linked costs will continue to affect freight rates, aviation, generator use, staff movement and supplier pricing, particularly where physical availability is constrained by refinery outages or disrupted transport routes.

### Situation

Physical disruption to refining and distribution remained the principal fuel-continuity issue during the reporting period. Russia was the clearest pressure point, with refinery restarts and official stabilisation statements offset by further strike-related disruption, continued local shortages and reports of imported petrol. Elsewhere, policy discussions in Australia, Pakistan and Nigeria focused on longer-term refinery capacity and fuel security but did not represent confirmed relief to near-term supply conditions.

### What Happened

Russian officials stated that disrupted refineries were returning to operation and that the domestic fuel situation was stabilising. However, the reported shutdown of the Tyumen refinery following a drone attack on 25 July, together with reports that Russia had imported petrol from India, showed that recovery was incomplete. The week therefore reflected a contested supply picture: refinery restarts reduced the risk of further immediate deterioration, while continued attacks, import dependence and uneven distribution sustained concerns over local availability.

### Operational Read

Refinery disruption and uneven product distribution remain the principal operational concerns. Local shortages and allocation measures may affect commercial users before changes are visible in national pump-price data.

Businesses should monitor refinery restart timelines, wholesale availability, import announcements and any force-majeure or allocation notices. Russia remains the main physical supply concern, while Ukraine continues to face fuel-distribution risk linked to damage, import dependence and transport disruption.

### Regional Highlights — Russia

Russia remains the principal fuel-supply pressure point. Refinery restarts have reduced the likelihood of immediate nationwide deterioration, but continuing drone attacks, local shortages and reported petrol imports indicate that normal supply has not been restored consistently. Businesses should monitor refinery operating status, wholesale availability and regional distribution conditions rather than relying solely on national stabilisation statements.

### Regional Highlights — Ukraine

Ukraine remains exposed to disruption affecting fuel storage, distribution and import routes. The principal concern is not domestic refining capacity alone, but the ability to maintain reliable product flows through road, rail and cross-border supply networks. Further damage to depots or transport infrastructure could quickly affect local availability and commercial movement.

### Gulf and Red Sea Chokepoint Watch

The Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb remained the principal international fuel-route risks during the reporting period. Reporting centred on attacks against tankers, reduced traffic through key routes and efforts to divert crude and product flows through alternative corridors. These developments increase transit times, insurance costs and the risk premium attached to regional fuel movements.

The most serious reported incident occurred on 29 July, when the Houthis claimed a ballistic-missile attack against a Saudi oil tanker in the Red Sea. Although separate from the Strait of Hormuz, the incident reinforced the wider vulnerability of regional energy routes and the limited availability of secure alternatives when more than one chokepoint is under pressure.

### What Matters

The immediate operational issue is the reliability of local fuel availability rather than headline crude-price movement. In Russia, transport scheduling, generator resilience and site access remain vulnerable to regional shortages and to the delay between announced refinery restarts and the restoration of normal distribution.

Policy announcements concerning new refinery capacity in Australia, Pakistan and Nigeria are strategically relevant but should not be treated as near-term supply relief. Current planning assumptions should remain based on confirmed operating capacity, wholesale availability and actual product flows.

### Polestar View

The near-term fuel-continuity outlook remains **Moderate**. Russia has moved away from straightforward deterioration, but the available reporting does not support a conclusion that supply has normalised. Official stabilisation statements should remain provisional until refinery operations, distribution flows and local availability show sustained improvement.

Internationally, the Gulf and Red Sea remain a separate but significant route-risk issue. Continued attacks or restrictions affecting Hormuz, the Red Sea or Bab el-Mandeb could increase freight, insurance and delivered-fuel costs even where underlying crude prices continue to ease.
