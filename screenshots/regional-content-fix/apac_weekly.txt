     REGIONAL OUTLOOK

     The week's most consequential exposure is the attack on the police headquarters in Kohat, where fatalities
     rose and fighting was still ongoing when reported. The immediate business risk is not a proven wider
     commercial shutdown, but an acute security shock around a state site that can interrupt staff movement,
     local site access and decision-making while conditions remain unsettled.

     A separate operating issue is the cluster of weather and infrastructure disruptions in Japan, Sri Lanka and
     Thailand, which directly affect transport reliability rather than security conditions. Nepal's LPG shortage and
     the Philippine regulator's offline platform add slower-moving friction through fuel availability and
     administrative processing, but with less evidence so far of broad service stoppage.

     REGIONAL RISK PICTURE

     Direct disruption this week comes through two different channels. In Kohat, the confirmed attack and
     ongoing fighting create an immediate local security constraint for employers with people, deliveries or
     public-facing activity near the affected area. That is a direct access and duty-of-care problem, not
     confirmed evidence of wider transport closure.

     Elsewhere, business interruption is being transmitted through transport corridors and public systems.
     Typhoon-related disruption in eastern Japan, a landslide on the Hatton Bogawantalawa Route and flooding
     that left part of Highway 401 impassable for small vehicles all slow staff movement and time-sensitive
     distribution where alternative routing is limited.

     The more contingent exposures are administrative and energy frictions. The LTFRB platform outage after a
     data breach can delay transactions that depend on the system, while Nepal's LPG shortage can constrain
     fuel-dependent operations if planned imports fail to ease availability.

     BUSINESS IMPLICATIONS

     Security and weather disruptions both require near-term movement decisions. In and around Kohat,
     employers should defer non-essential staff movement or meetings if operations touch the affected area
     while fighting remains ongoing. In eastern Japan and on disrupted routes in Sri Lanka and southern
     Thailand, transport teams should switch to route-by-route confirmation before dispatch or staff travel
     where heavy rain, landslide damage or flooding has already interrupted journeys.

     Road and fuel constraints warrant targeted continuity choices. Logistics managers with exposure to
     Highway 401 or the Hatton Bogawantalawa corridor should reroute loads that cannot tolerate local delays,
     triggered by confirmed impassability for small vehicles and route cut-off. In Nepal, fuel-dependent
     operators should review local LPG coverage and prioritise critical consumption only if the planned daily
     imports do not materially improve availability.

     The LTFRB platform outage creates a systems dependency issue rather than a proven transport stoppage.
     Firms needing regulator-facing submissions or platform-based transactions should identify any
     backlog-sensitive tasks and hold manual workarounds ready, triggered by continued offline status or
     confirmation that breach remediation is delaying access beyond routine interruption.
